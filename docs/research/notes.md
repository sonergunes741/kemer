# Kemer research notes

Compiled on 2026-11-11 by four research agents and checked by four independent fact-checkers. These notes ground the level data, the simulation constants and the in-game historical captions. Each section lists the facts as gathered, then the fact-check verdicts: prefer a corrected value over the original, and treat unverified items as approximate.


## Central and Eastern Anatolia: bridges and rivers

### 1. Ağırnas and Koramaz Valley (Kayseri) + Mimar Sinan biography

- Ağırnas lies in Melikgazi district, Kayseri, roughly 25 km (one source: ~34 km) east/southeast of Kayseri city, at about 38.82°N 35.72°E.
- The Koramaz Valley (with Ağırnas) was added to Turkey's UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List in 2020.
- Terrain is arid, volcanic and hard to farm; the valley walls are soft volcanic tuff, eroded and carved with Roman, Byzantine, Seljuk and Ottoman-era rock structures.
- Ağırnas has a carved-tuff underground city/tunnels (about 3,000 years of habitation) reaching the basement of Sinan's birthplace house.
- The south slope is a settlement of stone houses; the north slope has rock carvings.
- The village is known for dorak stone (yoghurt-straining stone) craft, practised nowhere else in Turkey.
- Tradition says Sinan carried spring water about 3 km to the village in clay pipes (about 500 years ago); this is a local claim and unverified.
- An Ottoman register of 1500 lists Koramaz as a nahiye to which Ağırnas belonged.
- Sinan's birth year: c. 1488 or 1490 in Ağırnas (sources give a range).
- Devşirme recruitment: 1511 (reign of Yavuz Selim), entered the Janissary corps.
- Appointed chief imperial architect (mimarbaşı): 1538, aged about 49.
- Died 17 July 1588 in Istanbul, aged 98 or 100.
- Büyükçekmece (Kanuni Sultan Süleyman) Bridge: built 1566-1567; Sinan lists it among the twelve bridges he built in his Tezkiretü'l Ebniye (autobiography).
- Other bridges credited to Sinan by library catalogues (not a verified inventory): Sokullu Mehmed Paşa Bridge, Lüleburgaz (1569-70), Silivri, Gebze, Alpullu (Sinanlı), Halkalıpınar; Küçükçekmece (c. 1560, 13 arches) per a Wikipedia-derived list.
- Sinan's Višegrad bridge (Sokolović Bridge, Drina) is attributed to him but page gave no year; temporary Prut bridge of 1538 was built in about ten days.
- Mostar Bridge is attributed to his student Mimar Hayreddin, not Sinan; Uzunköprü (Edirne, 1443) is not Sinan's.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: No source found for a named stream or any old small stone bridge in Ağırnas/Koramaz; stream name and local bridges are unverified. The Turkish Wikipedia Ağırnas page is flagged as unsourced. Sinan's birth year is a range; full authoritative list of his bridges (e.g. Bozkurt, Koca Sinan'ın Köprüleri) was not consulted; Büyükçekmece date range is 1566-67.

Sources: <https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Sinan>, <https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C4%9F%C4%B1rnas,_Melikgazi>, <https://www.cappadociahistory.com/post/koramaz-valley>, <https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/buyukcekmece-koprusu>, <https://dijitalkoleksiyonlar.kutuphane.itu.edu.tr/cdm/search/searchterm/Köprü>

### 2. Kızılırmak near Kayseri: Tekgöz and Şahruh bridges

- Kızılırmak is 1,355 km long with mean discharge about 128 m3/s (infobox, location of measurement unspecified).
- Near Kayseri the river is spring-flood prone, when rain and snowmelt combine (NTV, March 2024); no peak flood discharge figures for this reach were found.
- Tekgöz Bridge: Beydeğirmeni, Kocasinan, Kayseri, ~30 km NW of Kayseri, elevation 980 m.
- Tekgöz built 1203 per inscription, commissioned by Hacı Alişir of Kayseri for Seljuk sultan Süleyman II (1196-1204).
- Tekgöz length 120 m; one main pointed arch of 27 m span and 18 m height.
- Tekgöz has a smaller auxiliary arch (11.26 m span, 7.5 m high) used only at high water.
- Evliya Çelebi wrongly credited Süleyman the Magnificent with Tekgöz.
- Şahruh Bridge: Karaözü village, Sarıoğlan district, Kayseri, over the Kızılırmak.
- Şahruh length 144.5 m, width 5.6 m, 8 arches, widest span 12 m, built of stone.
- Şahruh was built by Şahruh, son of Dulkadirid Alaüddevle Bozkurt Bey, probably while sancakbeyi of Kırşehir; no construction inscription, so no later than 1480.
- Şahruh repair inscription dated 1538 by Mehmet Bey, son of Şahruh (reign of Süleyman I).
- Şahruh is still usable; in a recent high flow the 8 arches filled with water and the river had not risen that much in over 20 years.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Tekgöz stone type, repairs, current status not stated in fetched source; Şahruh date is a terminus ante quem (a view exists that it is Seljuk-era and restored by Şahruh). Setting/vegetation/water colour not verified (steppe, likely turbid).

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekg%C3%B6z_Bridge>, <https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Eahruh_K%C3%B6pr%C3%BCs%C3%BC>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%B1z%C4%B1l%C4%B1rmak_River>, <https://www.ntv.com.tr/turkiye/kizilirmakin-seviyesinde-ani-artis,G9ca-sqtHECwrKp11JX6lA>, <https://www.haberturk.com/kayseri-haberleri/40717831-kizilirmakin-debisi-yukselince-tarihi-sahruh-koprusunun-8-kemeri-suyla-doldu>

### 3. Diyarbakır: Dicle (On Gözlü) Bridge on the Tigris

- Located about 3 km south of Diyarbakır, outside Mardin Gate, over the Tigris (Dicle).
- Completed 1065 under the Kurdish Marwanid dynasty (patrons Nizam al-Din and Muyyid al-Dawla).
- Architect named in Kufic inscription on south facade: Yusuf son of Ubeyd son of Sandjar.
- Length 178 m, width about 5.60 m (varies).
- Ten arches; longest span 14.70 m.
- Built of black volcanic stone (basalt in the local Karacadağ lava-field context; source says 'black volcanic stones').
- Also called Silvan Bridge, On Gözlü Köprü, Pira Dehderî.
- Motor traffic ended in November 2009 when the new Marwanids Bridge opened about 1 km south.
- Tigris floods follow snowmelt in the Turkish mountains (April per Wikipedia); Baghdad mean flow 1,014 m3/s, max 2,779 m3/s (not local to Diyarbakır).

Confidence: medium. Caveats: No repair history or arch shape or pier cutwater details verified; no Diyarbakır flood discharge found. Pointed vs semicircular shape unverified.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicle_Bridge>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris>

### 4. Silvan: Malabadi Bridge on the Batman River

- Located near Malabadi village, Silvan, Diyarbakır, over the Batman Çayı (38.15N 41.20E).
- Batman River is 115 km long, rising in the Anti-Taurus near Sason and Genç; riverbed irregular, promoting flooding.
- Floods on the Batman and its tributary Iluh usually occur March-May, sometimes October-November; major floods 1969, 1972, 1991, 1995, 2006 (Oct 2006 flood killed 11).
- Batman Dam (1999) upstream regulates flow.
- Construction began 1146/47 (AH 541), completed about 1154 (AH 548/549) for Artuqid Husam al-Din Timurtash; he died Jan 1154 and Najm al-Din Alpi finished the arch.
- An earlier bridge reportedly collapsed in 1144 (Ibn al-Azraq); identification with Malabadi is uncertain.
- Building was hit by floods: eastern footing destroyed, builder al-Zahid bin al-Tawil fined for defective craftsmanship; Shirbarik restarted using massive timbers.
- Dimensions: 150 m long, 7 m wide, 19 m high.
- Single pointed main arch with clear span 38.6 m.
- Built of coloured solid masonry; approaches have small flood-relief arches; two piers in the river.
- Western support has two carved figures; two toll booths and small rooms in the main-arch spandrels.
- Restored late 12th century and in the early 20th century.
- In continuous use until the 1950s when a road bridge opened upstream; on Turkey's UNESCO Tentative List since 2016.

Confidence: high. Caveats: Span 38.6 m vs 40 m in some literature is possible; dating sources vary (1146/47 start; completion 1153/54 or c. 1154). Stone type not named beyond 'coloured'. Pier cutwater details not verified.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malabadi_Bridge>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batman_River>

### 5. Hasankeyf: Artuqid Old Bridge on the Tigris

- Spans the Tigris at Hasankeyf, Batman Province.
- Built by Artuqids, attributed by Ibn al-Azraq to Fakhr ad-Din Qara Arslan; built between about 1147 and 1167.
- An older 1116/17 date has lost support in recent scholarship.
- Four-arch bridge of stone masonry and rubble with some brick and wood; about 200 m abutment to abutment.
- Main arch span about 40 m, probably longest single span until Pont du Diable (1341).
- Two large piers in the river support the main arch; upstream faces carry relief carvings (five of an estimated eight survive).
- Restored under Ayyubid al-Adil Ghazi (1341-1367) and around 1473/74 under Aq Qoyunlu, with brick repairs on the surviving northern arch.
- A fallen arch rebuilt in wood is recorded in a 1673 account; an 1873 report says one arch fell in 1869.
- Ruined piers and one arch remain; since 2020 submerged by the Ilısu Dam reservoir.
- Four relief stones moved in 2018 to the Batman museum garden.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Sources disagree on date: 1116 vs 1147-1167; the 'collapsed before 1673' and 1869 fall are partly contradictory in how they are described. Flood discharge figures not found.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Bridge,_Hasankeyf>

### 6. Erzurum (Köprüköy/Pasinler): Çobandede Bridge on the Aras

- East of Köprüköy district, Erzurum, at a confluence of Aras tributaries on the Erzurum-Muş road.
- The Aras rises in the Bingöl Mountains at about 2,288 m, 1,072 km long, mean discharge 285 m3/s (whole river).
- Attributed (with a question mark) to Ilkhanid general Çoban; completed 1289 from a partly legible inscription.
- An 1865-copied inscription mentions an Armenian repair in 609 AH (1160) by a Magistros.
- Length 130 m; originally 7 arches, one now buried; six remain with spans 11.5, 13, 14.5, 15.5, 15.5, 15.5 m; abutments hold eight chambers.
- Cut stone in black, red and gray.
- Restored 1727, 1872 and 1946-48 (Turkish army).
- Out of service; D.955 traffic uses a parallel bridge about 500 m east.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Arch shape (pointed) not verified from source; Aras flood season/discharge at Köprüköy not found; Çoban attribution uncertain; 1160 repair predates 1289, implying an earlier bridge.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87obandede_Bridge>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aras_(river)>

### 7. Kars: Taşköprü on the Kars Çayı

- Three-arch stone bridge over the Kars River, northwest of the centre, directly south of Kars Castle.
- Built 1579 under Lala Mustafa Pasha (grand vizier the next year).
- A flood destroyed the original; rebuilt 1719 (some records 1725) by Karahanoğlu Hacı Ebubekir Bey.
- Length 53.5 m, width 8.40 m.
- Ashlar basalt blocks.
- Restored by Highways Directorate 18th Region in 2013; standing and a tourist attraction.
- The Kars River joins the Arpaçay (Akhuryan), a left tributary of the Aras.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Individual spans, arch shape and Kars River discharge not found; Turkish Wikipedia fetch failed. Date 1719 vs 1725 disagree.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_Bridge_(Kars)>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akhuryan_(river)>

### 8. Ani: Silk Road bridge on the Arpaçay (Akhurian)

- Ani sits on a triangular site with its east side defended by the Akhurian ravine; river now forms the closed Turkey-Armenia border.
- Akhuryan is 186 km long, basin 9,670 km2, rising at Lake Arpi (Armenia) at about 2,023 m, joining the Aras near Bagaran.
- Ani's structures use local volcanic basalt/tufa.
- Bridge: single circular (semicircular) arch of about 31 m, originally about 80 m long and 8 m wide (Turkish officials).
- Arch collapsed on 16 October 1807 per Turkish Transport Minister; only tall abutments/piers remain, possibly a fortified gate; a 19th-century guardhouse has vanished.
- Inscription indicates approach work in the early 14th century.
- Date disputed: Bagratuni (9th-11th century) vs more likely 13th century.
- On 4 May 2026 Türkiye and Armenia signed an agreement to rebuild the bridge.

Confidence: low. Caveats: Dating conflicts (9th, 10th, 13th century); 1807 collapse date and 31 m span from a minister's statement, not independently verified; no flood discharge found.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akhuryan_(river)>, <https://www.dailysabah.com/arts/ani-bridge-shared-heritage-set-for-revival-in-turkiye-armenia-pact/news>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ani>

### 9. Adıyaman: Cendere (Septimius Severus) Bridge

- Between Kahta and Sincik, Adıyaman, about 55 km NE of Adıyaman, near Arsameia, in Nemrut Dağı national park.
- Spans Cendere Çayı (Chabinas), a tributary of Kâhta Creek.
- Built about 200 CE under Septimius Severus.
- Single arch resting on two rocks at the creek's narrowest point; length 120 m, width 7 m.
- Clear span 34.2 m, longest known surviving Roman arch bridge.
- Four Corinthian columns 9-10 m tall; Geta's column removed after Caracalla had him killed.
- Built by Legio XVI Gallica.
- Restored 1997, vehicle loads limited to 5 tons; now closed to vehicles, new road bridge 500 m east.
- Used continuously from construction until the new bridge.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Stone type and arch rise not stated in the fetched source; no flood figures; Legio XVI Gallica was at Samosata per source.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cendere_Bridge>

## Thrace, Marmara, Black Sea and the south: bridges and rivers

### Uzunköprü (Ergene Nehri, Uzunköprü, Edirne)

- Crosses the Ergene River at Uzunköprü, Edirne Province (Thrace); built to carry troops and traffic over the Ergene's spring floods and wide marshy floodplain, replacing earlier wooden bridges that floods had destroyed.
- Architect: Muslihiddin (Muslihuddin) Usta, chief architect of Sultan II. Murad; Gazi Mahmud Bey supervised first, İshak Bey took over after his death; also a craftsman named Mehmed is credited.
- Build years: start 1426/27 (Hoca Sadeddin) or 1427/28 (Karaçelebizade), completed 1443/44; opened 1444 with the sultan attending (TR Wikipedia says 1427-1443).
- One TR source says an earlier 360-arch attempt begun about 1424 was rejected and demolished (single-source claim, unverified).
- Original: 174 arches, about 1,392 m long (some sources 1,393 m); largest arch about 14 m span.
- Present: 172 arches (one collapsed arch merged with neighbour); about 8 buried, so about 164 visible. Measured lengths vary: 1,266 m (1978), 1,254 m (1989), 1,306.2 m (2018); TR Wikipedia gives 1,238.55 m first to last arch.
- Width: about 5.24 m originally, widened to 6.80 m in the 1964-1971 restoration with a steel/concrete deck, which later caused severe damage.
- Height about 13.56 m; arches set high because of Ergene floods, with 7 drainage (relief) openings to reduce pressure on piers.
- Stone: cut stone quarried at Hasırcıarnavut (now in Greece), bonded with Horasan mortar; reliefs of lions, elephants, eagles, tulips and geometric motifs.
- Repairs recorded under Fatih Mehmed, II. Osman, II. Mahmud and II. Abdülhamid after floods and earthquakes (TR Wikipedia).
- Heavy vehicles banned 2013 after a new concrete bridge opened nearby.
- Major restoration began 2021 (closed to all traffic from about 20 Sept 2021); work on 25 arches, replacing asphalt with stone, unearthing buried piers. Originally 3-4 years; reported delayed to 2027 (EN Wikipedia, Feb 2024 delay); Minister inspected works 31 Jan 2025. 2027 date not independently confirmed.
- Applied for UNESCO tentative listing and Guinness (longest stone bridge) in 2017 (Daily Sabah).

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Length and arch counts differ widely between sources and survey years; restoration end date (2027) from one Wikipedia statement only. Ergene flood discharge figures and river regime not found in this research.

Sources: <https://tr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzunköprü>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzunköprü_Bridge>, <https://www.timeturk.com/dunya/tarihi-uzunkopru-restorasyon-calismalari-nedeniyle-trafige-kapatildi/haber-1702154>, <https://www.trthaber.com/haber/turkiye/bakan-uraloglu-tarihi-uzun-koprude-restorasyon-calismalarini-inceledi-896370.html>

### Edirne bridges on Tunca and Meriç (combined)

- Setting: Edirne sits at the confluence of the Tunca (Tundzha), Meriç (Maritsa) and Arda; Tunca and Meriç flow parallel for a short stretch before joining; flat alluvial plain in Thrace.
- Tunca is usually shallow but wide with a tendency to sudden floods; Yalnızgöz Bridge (Sinan, 1567; one main arch plus 7 small openings, mostly a dam wall) was built to regulate this.
- Saraçhane (Sahabettin Paşa) Bridge: 1451, named after Rumeli Beylerbeyi; 120 m long, 5 m wide, 10 arches of varied size.
- Fatih Bridge: 1452, Mehmed II; three slightly pointed arches, middle one larger.
- II. Bayezid Bridge: 1488, architect Mimar Hayreddin; 6 arches.
- Kanuni (Saray) Bridge: 1553/54, Süleyman I, Mimar Sinan; 4 slightly pointed arches, 65 m long with abutments (43 m without).
- Ekmekçizade Ahmed Paşa (Tunca) Bridge: built 1607-1615; 135 m, 8 arches, stone; 2 arches destroyed in WWII and replaced by temporary steel, restored to original form in 2007; carries D100 road.
- Meriç (Yeni) Bridge: built 1842-1847 under Abdülmecid (EN Wikipedia says opened 1842); 263 m; on the road to Western Thrace; replaced an earlier wooden bridge.
- Gazi Mihal Bridge: present stone bridge is 19th century, about 190 m, on the site of a 15th-century Mihal Bey bridge and a Roman bridge; a modern parallel bridge carries D100.
- Saraçhane and Kanuni bridges comprehensively restored in 2007.
- Flood behaviour: when the Meriç rises, the Tunca backs up at the confluence; in the February 2026 flood Tunca and Meriç bridges were closed to traffic and Yalnızgöz, Fatih and Kanuni bridges were partly submerged.
- Meriç flow reported: normal summer 30-40 m³/s, flood peak about 1,489 m³/s (later 1,447 m³/s); another report rose 384 to 1,119 m³/s in two days; a CNN Türk piece cites 2,149 m³/s as highest in 31 years (year unconfirmed).
- DSİ 'Kanal Edirne' relief canal was designed to divert part of flood flow away from the main Meriç channel (date not stated).

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Only a single English Wikipedia list was used for bridge dimensions; arch counts and lengths of Gazi Mihal, Meriç, Fatih and Bayezid not cross-checked; Wikipedia's Meriç year (1842) differs from search snippet (1842-1847). Flood date for 2026 event approximate (from search summary). Largest span, pier and cutwater details not found. One of the sources URL (turkgun) is as returned by search; spelling in this list may be 'turkgun.com'.

### Büyükçekmece Köprüsü (Kanuni Sultan Süleyman Köprüsü), Istanbul

- Crosses the outlet of Lake Büyükçekmece (a shallow lagoon) to the Marmara Sea, on the Istanbul-Rumelia road; low marshy ground.
- Built by Mimar Sinan for Süleyman I; construction started 1566 (some sources 1563), completed 1567 (TDV: 975 AH, 1567-68) under Selim II after Süleyman died on the Szigetvár campaign.
- Total length 635.57 m (TDV; EN Wikipedia 636 m); a local newspaper gave about 700 m; width 7.17 m.
- 28 pointed arches in four separate humpback sections (babalar/pier towers at section ends): section 1: 7 arches 157.23 m; section 2: 7 arches 135.10 m; section 3: 5 arches 101.25 m; section 4: 9 arches 183.72 m; gaps between sections 25.41 m, 15.52 m, 17.34 m (flood water can pass).
- Smallest span 4.50 m; largest span 11.86 m (fourth section).
- Foundations: per TDV, Sinan used caisson-like cofferdams per pier, drove piles inside them and poured lead to join parts; EN Wikipedia says lake water was pumped out during construction and about 40,000 m³ of stone placed.
- Earlier bridge: Roman/Byzantine Athyras bridge with guard towers, on the Via Egnatia, ruined by 1433 (Bertrandon de la Broquière), repaired by Mehmed II; lay north of Sinan's bridge in the marsh and failed when floods pushed its foundations into the marsh.
- Sinan chose a much longer crossing sited further south across the whole marsh.
- Soon after completion floods damaged some arches; Sinan repaired them.
- Evliya Çelebi (1651) counted 26 arches (TDV regards this as an error).
- 1960s: treasure hunters broke the inscription kiosk section bearing Sinan's name; later rebuilt. Restored 1970 and 1986-1989.
- Present: traffic moved to a modern wider bridge on the seaward side; historic bridge kept as a monument (stone type not stated in sources, built of stone).

Confidence: high. Caveats: Start year 1563 (one summary) vs 1566 (EN Wikipedia). Stone type and colour not stated in fetched text. 'Soft ground' inferred from marsh/lagoon setting and pile foundations, not explicit.

Sources: <https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/buyukcekmece-koprusu>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanuni_Sultan_Suleiman_bridge_(Istanbul)>

### Justinianus (Beşköprü / Sangarius) Köprüsü, Sakarya

- Location: Serdivan, Sakarya; today it spans the small Çark Deresi, while the wide Sakarya River lies about 3 km east.
- Built under Justinian I, completed 562 AD (Theophanes dates start to 559-560; may have begun about 554).
- Length 429-430 m including abutments (EN Wikipedia); Turkish press gives 365, 370, 384 or 429 m.
- Width 9.85 m, up to about 10 m high.
- Seven main arches: five central spans of 23-24.5 m and a smaller flanking arch of about 20 m each side; plus five floodway arches of 3-9 m on the banks (EN Wikipedia). Turkish sources often say 12 arches.
- Material: limestone blocks with Horasan (pozzolanic) mortar.
- Damage: eastern part partly destroyed when a railway was built along the river; crosses on piers destroyed; western triumphal arch lost; inscription lost (text preserved in Constantine VII).
- Restoration started 2018 and lasted about 8 years; reopened to visitors (per DHA/NTV reports); on Turkey's UNESCO tentative list.
- Function debated: may relate to a canal/harbour project of Justinian rather than only river crossing.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Arch count conflicts (7 main + 5 flood arches vs 12 total in Turkish sources, which may be the same count) and length conflicts (365-429 m). Arch shape not stated (Roman bridges usually semicircular, unverified). River course change and flood data not found.

### Fırtına Deresi bridges, Çamlıhemşin, Rize (Şenyuva and others)

- Fırtına River (Peruma) is 68 km long, rises in the Kaçkar Mountains and flows to the Black Sea near Ardeşen; steep-sided, forested valley (Çamlıhemşin, Çat Vadisi), frequently flooded, which is why the arches are high.
- More than 20 Ottoman-era stone bridges survive along the river.
- Şenyuva (Cin Civa) Bridge: single arch, about 40 m long and about 20 m above the stream (press figures); arch of dressed stone, piers of rubble stone; dated 1696 (or 1699) by an inscription that was lost in the 1946 flood.
- Şenyuva bridge restoration work reported in 2019; vegetation growing between stones cited as damage cause.
- Other bridges: Çamlıhemşin Bridge (undated), Kaptanpaşa Bridge (18th/19th-c. style), Buzlupınar (19th c.), Köprüköy (19th c.), Mikron (mid-19th c.; arches and fill repaired 1998), Ardeşen (late 19th c., poor condition, dressed and rough stone), Zil Kale/Hala and others.
- Regime: rain-fed and snowmelt Black Sea river with high rainfall; typical flood season autumn/spring-early summer (general knowledge, not verified in sources). Discharge figures not found.
- Water colour typically clear to milky-green/white whitewater (general knowledge, not source-verified).

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Span, rise and pier details for other bridges not found; Şenyuva dimensions from press, not surveys. No flood discharge figures found; the often-cited 2010 flood was not confirmed in sources.

### Adana: Taşköprü (Seyhan) and Misis Köprüsü (Ceyhan)

- Taşköprü (Stone Bridge) over the Seyhan River in central Adana: Roman, probably first half of 2nd century AD, usually attributed to Hadrian (some sources Pompey/Augustus era).
- Length today 310 m (Evliya Çelebi recorded about 170 m; another table gives 291 m); width 11.4 m including sidewalks; shortened because approach arches were buried during riverbank regulation.
- Arches: originally 21; present 15 primary and 6 relieving arches per EN Wikipedia (Spanish summary of Turkish sources: 14 visible, 7 buried); late-Ottoman yearbook says 22.
- Materials: mainly tufa and marble plus spolia; Ottoman restoration rebuilt upstream cutwaters.
- Documented repairs: Justinian I (Procopius, about 557, piers battered by current below waterline); Umayyad 742 (Jisr al-Walid); 840 al-Mu'tasim; Ottoman 1713 (Ahmed III), 1847 (Abdülmecid), Abdülhamid II; cobble paving 1899-1908; State Highway restoration 1948.
- Deck was about 3 m wide originally; widened in early 20th century with new arches downstream.
- Status: closed to motor vehicles in 2007; pedestrians and events only.
- Misis Bridge over the Ceyhan River at Misis, Yüreğir, Adana: built 4th century under Constantius II, restored under Justinian I in 6th century, renovated 743 and 840.
- Misis: 9 arches, about 132.70 m by 6.50 m (comparison table, origin not stated), ashlar stone.
- Misis damaged in the 1998 Adana-Ceyhan earthquake, restored, and remains open to vehicles and pedestrians (press).
- Setting: Çukurova delta plain with Seyhan and Ceyhan fed by Taurus snowmelt and rain (general knowledge); flood discharge figures not found.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Taşköprü arch count conflicts (21 vs 14 visible vs 22) and length (310 vs 291 m). Misis length from one unsourced table, Misis arch spans, shape and flood data missing; the Wikipedia Taşköprü URL path may differ (Stone_Bridge_(Adana)). Seyhan and Ceyhan flood discharges and regime not verified.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taşköprü_(Adana)>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misis_Bridge>, <https://kureansiklopedi.com/tr/detay/adana-tas-kopru>, <https://www.gazetevatan.com/galeri/lokman-hekimin-olumsuz-ismini-verdigi-antik-kent-misis-2147938>

### Aspendos (Köprüçay / Eurymedon) bridge, Antalya

- Crosses the Eurymedon (Köprüçay) near Aspendos (Serik, Antalya), Pamphylia plain.
- Roman bridge dated 4th century AD (nearby aqueduct still working then); may have been rebuilt after the May 363 earthquake; 259.50 m long, 9.44 m wide, 9 semicircular arches, central span 23.52 m clear, flanking arches 14.95 m, roughly perpendicular to river.
- Roman piers protected by masonry reinforcement and wedge-shaped breakwaters; river normally flowed through the three central arches.
- Roman bridge collapsed (probably earthquake); Seljuk Sultan Kayqubad I (1219-1237) built a new bridge on its remains (Köprüpazar Köprüsü), a stone bridge reusing Roman blocks and about 250 pipe stones from the aqueduct.
- Seljuk bridge has a sharp zigzag layout following old pier remains, arches about 4.1 m lower than Roman, width almost halved; one source gives about 220 m with 7 arches (not confirmed).
- Crumbling breastwork restored in the late 1990s, revealing Greek and Arabic inscriptions.
- Status: Seljuk bridge still standing; Roman bridge survives only as remains.
- Setting: Taurus-fed river with snowmelt and rain regime, discharge figures not found.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Seljuk bridge length and arch count conflict (220 m, 7 arches unverified); seljuk arch shape (pointed?) not confirmed. River regime and flood figures not researched. Do not confuse with the small Roman Eurymedon (Oluk) bridge near Selge/Beşkonak.

Sources: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurymedon_Bridge>, <https://www.ancient-history-sites.com/sites/eurymedon-bridge/>, <https://structurae.net/de/bauwerke/seldschukische-eurymedonbruecke>

### Tokat Hıdırlık Köprüsü (Yeşilırmak) and Sivas Eğri Köprü (Kızılırmak)

- Hıdırlık Köprüsü (Yeşilırmak Köprüsü), Tokat: built 1250 in the Seljuk period (said to reconcile the three sons of Keyhüsrev II); the only Seljuk bridge preserved with its inscription.
- Hıdırlık: about 151 m long, 7 m wide, 5 pointed arches of cut stone (KÜRE gives about 88 m and 5.5 m; 151 m more common).
- Hıdırlık survived recent floods in which two modern Tokat bridges collapsed (per university expert).
- Hıdırlık closed to vehicles in 2013 after alternative bridges; pedestrian only; asphalt removed in 2021 restoration exposing original stones.
- Eğri Köprü, Sivas: over the Kızılırmak about 3 km southeast of the city; probably Seljuk (1174-1243 uncertain), no dated inscription; rebuilt extensively in the late Ottoman period.
- Eğri Köprü: 179.60 m long, 4.55 m wide (173 m in some press), 18 pointed arches, most of equal width and 3 notably wider; dressed ashlar stone with reused stonework.
- Eğri Köprü bends 45 degrees: about 60 m straight over first 6 arches, then about 120 m over the remaining 12; convex angle faces the flow, perhaps against ice pressure; steeper over last 3 arches.
- Piers have triangular cutwaters; parapet gaps drain rainwater.
- Repairs: 1585 (Murad III), 1217 AH (1802/03 or 1807/08), 19th century under governors Halil Rıfat Paşa and Kangal Abdurrahman Paşa; 2006 KGM restoration (stone now pale); registered 1983; pedestrian only since a modern bridge was built about 50 m upstream.
- Kızılırmak (Turkey's longest river) regime: snowmelt-fed with spring peak (general knowledge, not verified); no flood discharge figures found.

Confidence: high. Caveats: Hıdırlık length conflict 88 vs 151 m; Eğri Köprü length 173 vs 179.6 m; repair year 1802/03 vs 1807/08 (both given as 1217 AH, which corresponds to 1802/03; one source says 1807-1808). Largest span and pier details for Hıdırlık not found. River flood discharges not found.

Sources: <https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/770-year-old-tokat-bridge-defies-time-222648>, <https://kureansiklopedi.com/tr/detay/yesilirmak-koprusu-a8c4c>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bent_Bridge>, <https://kureansiklopedi.com/tr/detay/egri-kopru-sivas-d4b25>

## Hazards, traffic loads through the ages, and the craft

### 1. Major historical earthquakes in Anatolia/Thrace relevant to stone bridges

- 1268/1269 Cilicia (Adana-Kozan area, about 37.5N 35.5E): about M7 (estimated), about 60,000 deaths in the list; the date is given as 1268 by some sources and 14 May 1269 by Wikipedia's list.
- 1509 Istanbul (10 Sep 1509, 'Küçük Kıyamet'): Mw about 7.2; deaths estimated 1,000 to 13,000 (one source says 130,000, unreliable). Significant damage (above intensity VII) from Çorlu in the west to İzmit in the east.
- 1509: damage was reported by letter even from Edirne, Çorlu, Gelibolu and Dimetoka; Bayezid II stayed 10 days in a tent in the palace garden, then moved to Edirne. So Edirne/Thrace felt it strongly.
- 1668 North Anatolia (17 Aug 1668): largest recorded quake in Turkey, Ms about 7.8-8.0, max intensity IX, about 8,000 deaths. Ruptured the North Anatolian Fault over a very long stretch (Bolu to Erzincan region).
- 1766 Istanbul/Marmara: 22 May 1766, Ms about 7.1, about 4,000 deaths; a second Marmara shock on 5 Aug 1766 is rated about Mw 7.4. Both hit the Istanbul and Marmara/Thrace side.
- 1784 Erzincan (18 Jul 1784): Ms about 7.6, widespread damage between Erzincan and Muş (Eastern Anatolia).
- 1859 Erzurum (2 Jun 1859): M about 6.1, about 15,000 deaths per Wikipedia-derived list (Erzurum/Kars side).
- 1894 Istanbul (10 Jul 1894, Gulf of İzmit/Marmara): M about 7.0, about 1,300 deaths; strongest impact in Istanbul, Yalova, Kocaeli.
- 1939 Erzincan (27 Dec 1939): Mw 7.8, max intensity XII per Wikipedia summary. Death toll about 33,000 is commonly cited but was NOT verified in this session.
- 1966 Varto (19 Aug 1966, Muş): M 6.7-6.8; deaths 2,394 (list) or 2,529 (other source).
- 1975 Lice (6 Sep 1975, Diyarbakır): Mw/Ms 6.6-6.7 (USGS 6.7, Kandilli 6.6 Ms); about 2,385 deaths, 8,149 houses heavily damaged; aftershocks for 3-4 months. Directly affected Diyarbakır province (Lice is about 70-80 km from Diyarbakır, distance not verified); about 80% of Diyarbakır's current building stock post-dates 1975.
- 1983 Erzurum-Kars (30 Oct 1983, Horasan): M 6.9; deaths 1,155 (Habertürk), about 1,340 (HDN) or 1,342 (list).
- 1999 İzmit (17 Aug 1999): Mw 7.4-7.6 (sources differ), about 17,127 deaths; Marmara/Kocaeli/Sakarya, strongly felt in Istanbul.
- 2023 Kahramanmaraş (6 Feb 2023): first shock Mw 7.8 near Nurdağı, second shock about Mw 7.6 (some agencies give 7.5-7.7) hours later; death toll not verified in this session (widely reported above 50,000, unverified here). Diyarbakır was affected (earthquake survivors' tent city set up there).
- Strong-felt mapping (only partly verified): Istanbul/Edirne/Thrace: 1509, 1766, 1894, 1999. Erzurum/Kars: 1784 Erzincan-Muş, 1859, 1983, also 1939 and 1966 (regional, unverified). Diyarbakır/Silvan: 1975 Lice, 2023 (verified for Diyarbakır). Kayseri: no source found in this session for any of these; Cilicia 1268/69 and 2023 plausible but unverified.
- Silvan/Ongözlü (Diyarbakır) specific damage from any quake: not found.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Magnitudes for pre-1900 events are catalogue estimates; death tolls disagree between sources (1509: 1,000-130,000; 1966: 2,394 vs 2,529; 1983: 1,155-1,342). The 1939 death toll, 2023 toll and all Kayseri felt-intensity claims were not verified. Cilicia date 1268 vs 1269.

### 2. Documented floods and flood-damaged stone bridges

- Büyükçekmece: the older bridge at the site was damaged/destroyed by flooding; one source dates the destruction to 20 Sep 1563 (single secondary source). Eyyûbî's Menâkıb-ı Sultan Süleyman says the old bridge was damaged by floods and a decree ordered its renewal.
- TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi version: Kanuni ordered a new bridge after seeing the old one in ruins. Sinan argued the old (Roman-era) bridge failed because its foundation sat on marsh, and built the new one further south on firmer ground.
- Kanuni Sultan Süleyman (Büyükçekmece) Bridge: built 1563/1566 to 1567; 28 arches, about 636 m long; restored 1986-1989.
- Edirne: sits on the floodplain of Arda, Meriç and Tunca; a Trakya University study notes most 16th-century Edirne floods were on the Tunca, which divides the city and has many bridges.
- Edirne: floods in 1931-1941 are studied in a DergiPark article; a flood-table date of 11 Jan 1955 appears in another paper (unverified details); February 2026 flooding on Meriç and Tunca closed historic bridges in Edirne.
- Uzunköprü (Ergene river, not the Meriç): one upstream mill was destroyed in the 1956 flood (Kure Ansiklopedi). Bridge: built 1426-1443, repaired in Ottoman times and in 1928, 1963/1964 and 1972.
- Tigris basin: flood of 31 Oct to 2 Nov 2006 killed 42 people (MGM), with Diyarbakır and Batman hardest hit. Tigris basin is second after the East Black Sea basin for flood deaths in Turkey.
- Diyarbakır: HEC-RAS study (Oğraş 2018) found the historic On Gözlü Köprü (Onbirgöz) and Sadi Köprüsü hydraulically under-sized (insufficient openings). March 2023 flood (15 Mar) inundated a tent city on the Dicle bank.
- Batman Çayı/Malabadi: Turkish Wikipedia reports the earlier Akarman/Karaman bridge built under the Artuqid Timurtaş was destroyed by a flood about two years after completion (no exact date). Modern Malabadi bridge (1955) built Dec 1954 to Mar 1955. Batman Çayı meanders and shifts channel, repeated flood losses (Dicle Univ. study 2015).
- Seyhan (Adana): flood embankments built 1953; Seyhan Dam in service 1956; major flood 27 Mar to 6 Apr 1980 inundated about 28,236 ha in the Seyhan-Berdan area.
- Not found: dated historical floods for Kızılırmak, Fırtına Deresi, Aras, Tigris before 2006, and named stone bridges destroyed on them. Do not invent; leave as design-level random events.

Confidence: low. Caveats: Only the Büyükçekmece, Seyhan 1980, Tigris 2006 and Uzunköprü 1956 items are reasonably sourced; Büyükçekmece flood date rests on one secondary source and the 1563 vs 1566 start year differs. Requested Kızılırmak, Fırtına, Aras, Tunca/Meriç historical flood years and 1896/1902/1934 Edirne dates could not be verified.

### 3. Traffic loads through the ages

## Engineering benchmarks: limit analysis and hydraulics

### 1. Heyman limit analysis of masonry arches

- Heyman's three assumptions: (1) masonry has no tensile strength, (2) masonry has unlimited (infinite) compressive strength, (3) sliding between voussoirs cannot occur.
- With these assumptions the arch is a rigid-block system and failure is a mechanism of rotating hinges (no crushing, no sliding).
- Safe (lower-bound / static) theorem: if ANY line of thrust in equilibrium with the loads can be found lying entirely within the masonry profile, the arch will not collapse.
- The safe theorem needs no knowledge of the actual abutment movements, so cracking and settlements do not invalidate the safe statement.
- Uniqueness theorem: collapse load is found when a mechanism and a thrust line touching the boundary at the hinge points coincide; the true collapse load is bracketed by upper (kinematic) and lower (static) bounds.
- Geometric factor of safety (Heyman): GFS = actual thickness t / minimum thickness t_min needed to contain a thrust line (for the same shape and loads).
- If GFS > 1 the arch is safe by the safe theorem; practice: GFS is often >2 for real arches.
- GFS = 3 corresponds to being able to fit a thrust line inside the middle third of the ring (a section kept entirely in compression).
- Common practical acceptance values: GFS >= 2 is a frequently quoted comfortable value for existing arches; a GFS of 1.0 to 1.5 means the arch is near its geometric limit. (the exact code-recommended values were not retrieved, see caveats)
- Later critiques note the assumptions neglect finite compressive strength, sliding and tensile bond; Tempesta and Galassi propose a 'full-range' performance factor instead of the pure geometric factor.
- Game use: compute GFS = t / t_min(geometry, load) and treat GFS < 1 as collapse and GFS 1 to 1.5 as at risk.

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Assumptions, safe theorem and GFS definition and the middle-third interpretation were found in search summaries. The 'GFS >= 2 recommended' value is common engineering knowledge and was not verified in a retrieved source; no code value (e.g. Eurocode/Italian code) was verified.

### 2. Minimum thickness of circular and semicircular arches under self-weight

- Semicircular arch of minimum thickness: Milankovitch (1907) and Ochsendorf (2002) t/R = 0.1075 with intrados hinges at beta = 54.5 degrees from the crown (Milankovitch hinge 54.48 deg).
- Heyman (1969) gave t/R = 0.1060 with hinges at 58.9 degrees from the crown (58.83 deg in Cocchetti et al.); this is about 1.4% too thin (unsafe).
- Petit (1835) gave t/R = 0.1078 (+0.3% vs 0.1075); Couplet (1730) and Heyman's values are slightly smaller than the true minimum, i.e. unsafe.
- Cocchetti-Colasante-Rizzi (CCR) corrected Heyman solution: t/R = 0.107426 at 54.50 deg; Milankovitch exact solution 0.107478 at 54.484 deg (semicircle).
- R in these values is the CENTRELINE (mid-thickness) radius (Ochsendorf: 'circular segment with a centreline radius of R'). Converting: t/R_intrados = (t/R)/(1 - t/(2R)) = 0.1136 for 0.1075.
- Hinge pattern at minimum thickness for a semicircle: 5 hinges, intrados hinges at about 54.5 deg each side of the crown, extrados hinge at the crown, extrados hinges at the springings (haunch hinges intrados, crown and springing extrados).
- Minimum t/R vs half-angle of embrace alpha (Ochsendorf 2002, Table 5.2, centreline R, own weight only): alpha 40 deg -> 0.0047 (hinge 27.4 deg); 50 -> 0.0113 (33.6); 60 -> 0.0228 (39.5); 70 -> 0.0413 (45.0); 80 -> 0.0687 (49.9); 90 -> 0.1075 (54.5).
- Hinge angle beta in that table is the intrados hinge location measured from the crown.
- Cocchetti/Colasante/Rizzi 2010 (Heyman / CCR / Milankovitch) for larger arches: half-angle 60 deg: t/R about 0.0228 (hinge 39.5-40.8 deg); half-angle 90 deg: 0.106-0.1075; half-angle 120 deg (240 deg total embrace): t/R about 0.31-0.33; half-angle 145 deg: roughly 0.66-0.76 (row-to-method assignment in the extracted table is garbled).
- Approximation of Ochsendorf: (t/R)min ~ alpha^4/48 with alpha in radians (safe estimate); inverted, alpha_max = (48 t/R)^(1/4).
- Check of approximation: t/R = 0.0228 gives alpha_max = 58.6 deg vs exact 60 deg.
- Segmental arches below about 80 deg total embrace need almost no thickness (t/R < 0.005 at 80 deg total).
- Pointed arches: no published minimum t/R values were found in this search; test numerically with a thrust-line solver (the semicircle values above are the baseline).

### 3. Collapse under a point load (four-hinge mechanism, test cases)

- Classic mechanism for a point load on a segmental or semicircular arch: four hinges alternating intrados/extrados, e.g. hinge under the load (intrados side opposite... extrados at the load), one near the abutment on the loaded side, one on the far side near the opposite quarter, and one at the far springing.
- The minimum collapse load occurs when the load is near quarter span (search literature: failure load is lowest 'at quarter or third span'); in tests, load lines are placed at 1/4 or 1/3 span.
- Reference: Prestwood Bridge (Staffordshire and Worcestershire Canal) was a brick segmental arch with 220 mm ring thickness, loaded at quarter span across the full width; it collapsed as a four-hinge mechanism.
- Prestwood collapse load: the TRRL abstract says 228 kN; one other source says 1325 kN maximum applied, (conflicting, see caveats).
- Preston Bridge (Shropshire), the companion TRRL test, was loaded at one-third span.
- Gilbert/Melbourne (Sheffield) lab bridges for software validation: full-scale 3 m span, 750 mm rise, 215 mm barrel thickness; small-scale 380 mm span, 85 mm rise, 28 mm thickness; load applied at quarter span; tests of 3 m and 5 m span multi-ring bridges.
- A nominally identical bridge tested with attached spandrel walls failed at a lower load (320 kN) than the test with detached walls (from Gilbert's work).
- With a higher joint friction coefficient the DMEM study found four flexural hinges, except central load which produced a five-hinge mechanism.
- Four-hinge formation is a mechanism for the collapse load; for serviceability, cracking begins much earlier.
- Ochsendorf-style closed-form self-weight cases in entries 2 and 4 (minimum thickness 0.1075, tilt table) are cleaner unit-test cases than the point-load examples, whose fill/span data could not be retrieved.

### 4. Collapse under horizontal ground acceleration (tilt analysis)

- Oppenheim (1992) modeled a circular arch as a four-link mechanism under horizontal base acceleration (Lagrangian equation of motion, numerically integrated) and found ground acceleration must exceed a fairly high threshold before the mechanism moves.
- Ochsendorf (2002) used tilt analysis: horizontal acceleration lambda*g plus gravity equals tilted gravity at angle gamma from vertical, so lambda = tan(gamma).
- Collapse acceleration is the minimum lateral acceleration to form a four-hinge mechanism; below it the arch moves as a rigid body.
- Circular arch, centreline radius R, thickness t, total embrace 2*alpha, self-weight only.
- Ochsendorf Table 6.1: minimum-thickness arches, hinge A forms at the support when t/R, alpha_max and lambda are: t/R 0.011: alpha 50 deg, gamma 8.0 deg, lambda 0.14; 0.023: 60 deg, 10.0 deg, 0.18; 0.041: 70 deg, 12.5 deg, 0.22; 0.069: 80 deg, 15.0 deg, 0.27; 0.108: 90 deg, 17.5 deg, 0.32.
- Rule of thumb: gamma ~ alpha_max - alpha (slope -1 in the alpha-gamma plot), with alpha_max = (48 t/R)^(1/4) in radians.
- Worked example (Ochsendorf): total embrace 130 deg (alpha = 65 deg = 1.13 rad), t/R = 0.09: approximate gamma = 18 deg (lambda 0.32 g), exact ArchTilt result gamma = 21 deg (lambda 0.38 g). The approximation is conservative.
- For typical arches (t/R 0.04-0.10, alpha 60-80 deg) collapse acceleration is below 0.5 g, within strong-earthquake peak ground accelerations.
- Semicircular arch of minimum thickness: critical intrados hinges about 55 deg from the crown (a = 35 deg, b = 145 deg measured from horizontal) in the tilted mechanism.
- The threshold acceleration is not a failure criterion for short pulses: an arch can survive higher peak acceleration if it lasts briefly; DeJong, De Lorenzis and Ochsendorf (2007) found collapse under impulses can occur at lower accelerations than previously thought (dynamic rocking).
- Clemente (1998) studied the influence of geometry on the hinge pattern, assuming hinges at the supports, and Ochsendorf notes this misses the two-hinge-in-span mechanisms.
- Rule for game: use quasi-static lambda from the table as the earthquake capacity check, with a dynamic reduction/pulse factor (not quantified here).

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Table 6.1 and the 130-degree example are directly from Ochsendorf's thesis. Oppenheim's reference arch (radius/thickness/angle) and Clemente's tabulated values were NOT retrieved, and a published DeJong reference arch value was not found. The 0.1 g statement that appeared in my search query is NOT verified.

### 5. Support movement and abutment spreading

- Ochsendorf (2002 thesis, 2006 The Structural Engineer vol 84 issue 2): a rigid-block arch can tolerate abutment spread until a five-hinge mechanism forms with a maximum thrust that grows with spread.
- Span increase to collapse is approximately linear in the thickness ratio t/R for circular arches.
- Worked example from the thesis: circular arch subtending 120 deg (alpha = 60 deg) with t/R = 0.10 fails at a span increase of about 8% of the span, with thrust about 2.17 times the minimum thrust.
- The thesis chart of maximum span increase tops out near 24% of span for thick, wide arches (t/R up to 0.24); the real value depends on embrace angle and is read off Figure 5.14.
- A minimum-thickness arch (t/R at the Table 5.2 value) has zero tolerance for additional spread.
- During spread the intrados hinge moves from its initial angle toward the crown; smaller embrace angles see larger hinge movement.
- The analysis assumes the thrust at the abutment stays at minimum thrust until the intrados hinges reach their critical positions; collapse then occurs by a five-hinge symmetrical mechanism.
- The practical thrust cap in the analysis is 5 times the minimum thrust.
- A case in the thesis notes a historical arch increased its span by 370 mm (nearly 7% of its span) (context line only, not analysed here).
- Game use: abutment spread (scour, settlement, earthquake) as a percentage of span compared with the thickness-dependent limit; limit ~ 2-4% for t/R ~0.05 and ~8% for t/R 0.10 at 120 deg embrace (the 2-4% figure is my interpolation, unverified).

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Only the 8% example is a printed number; the 24% maximum is the chart axis limit. A table of spread/span versus t/R and embrace was not retrieved (figures only). The 2-4% for t/R 0.05 is an unverified interpolation; do not cite.

### 6. Empirical design rules and span/rise, pier/span ratios

- Empirical rules give crown thickness from span or rise (or radius) because mechanics was not applied before the 18th-19th centuries.
- A 59-bridge comparison found reasonable agreement between the historical rules and real bridges except for the proposals of Alberti and Gautier.
- A lecture example in the Scribd 'Design of Masonry Arches' notes: for a 12 m span semicircular arch with 6 m rise the rules give crown thickness from about 539 mm (Hurst) to 712 mm (Trautwine).
- Chestofbooks (Safe Building) rule: crown depth = c * sqrt(radius) with c = 0.30 for cut stone in blocks, 0.40 for brickwork, 0.45 for rubble (units not specified there, check).
- Rankine's rule (from memory, unverified): crown thickness = sqrt(0.12 r) with r the crown radius in feet.
- Alberti (1/10 of span), Perronet, Dejardin and Sejourne formulas could not be verified in the retrieved sources and are not given as numbers.
- Roman bridges: usually semicircular, some segmental; span to rise: Trajan's Bridge about 7:1, Limyra Bridge average 5.3:1, San Lorenzo Bridge 3.7:1.
- Roman pier thickness: common about one third of the span, some as slim as one fifth (Ponte Altinate about 1:5) and one eighth (Ponte San Lorenzo, 1.70 m piers).
- Karamagara Bridge in Cappadocia (late Roman, 17 m span) may be the earliest surviving pointed-arch bridge (now submerged by the Keban Dam).
- Malabadi Köprüsü (Batman Çayı, Artuklu, built 541-548 AH, about 1146-1154): main pointed arch 38.6 m span, 19 m overall height, 7 m width, 150 m length; some sources give 40.86 m / 24.5 m.
- Seljuk and Ottoman pier-to-span ratios were not found; Ottoman bridges commonly use pointed or semicircular arches with relatively thick piers and a cutwater, but numeric ratios are unverified.

Confidence: low. Caveats: Most requested formulas (Alberti, Perronet, Dejardin, Sejourne) were NOT verified; a 2025 catalogue by Michela Monaco and the Salford/Chalmers papers likely hold them. Rankine formula from memory. Malabadi data: Wikipedia 38.6 m / 19 m versus a travel blog 40.86 m / 24.5 m and the Turkish encyclopedia entry flags itself as AI-assisted.

Sources: <https://es.scribd.com/document/100627524/Design-of-Mansonry-Arches>, <https://chestofbooks.com/architecture/Safe-Building/Arches-Part-7.html>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponte_Altinate>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malabadi_Bridge>, <https://kureansiklopedi.com/tr/detay/malabadi-koprusu-c6d9d>

### 7. Material data (stone, masonry, fill)

- Basalt: density about 2800 kg/m3; intact compressive strength 110-338 MPa (very wide); a code-style crushing strength for basalt stone masonry quoted as 8.5 N/mm2.
- Limestone: compressive strength about 55 MPa for typical building limestone; specific gravity 2.0-2.75.
- Sandstone: specific gravity about 2.89 (some sources); compressive strength 60-70 MPa; generally below 100 MPa.
- Volcanic tuff (lapilli tuff): bulk density 1815 kg/m3 and UCS 8 MPa; a less welded unit 1635 kg/m3 and 0.35 MPa; historic tuff masonry stones mean about 2-5.7 MPa.
- Masonry (as built wall/barrel) strength is much lower than intact rock; use masonry compressive strength of a few MPa to about 10 MPa for rubble/tuff and 10-30 MPa for good ashlar (from general knowledge, unverified).
- Typical joint friction: tan(phi) about 0.6-0.7 for dry or mortared stone joints (commonly used default 0.6; unverified here).
- Unit weight of masonry: dense limestone/basalt ashlar 25-28 kN/m3, tuff 16-18 kN/m3, sandstone 22-26 kN/m3 (derived from densities above).
- Fill: compacted earth/rubble fill about 18-21 kN/m3 (RING style default values, unverified here).
- Poly Haven type values (generic) agree with density values above.

Confidence: low. Caveats: Values come from commercial/general sources; friction coefficient 0.6-0.7 and fill unit weight 18-21 kN/m3 were NOT found in any retrieved source and are from engineering practice. Intact vs masonry strength differ by a factor 5-20. Use ranges, not single values.

Sources: <https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/18/583/2018/nhess-18-583-2018-t03.pdf>, <https://www.ausimm.com/globalassets/insights-and-resources/minerals-processing-toolbox/rockprop-1.pdf>, <https://theconstructor.org/building/building-material/commonly-used-building-stones-properties-uses/>, <https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/amp/stone-masonry-strength-d_1833.html>

### 8. Bridge hydraulics (scour, drag, afflux, buoyancy, failure sequence)

- HEC-18 (CSU) pier scour: ys/y1 = 2.0 * K1 * K2 * K3 * (a/y1)^0.65 * Fr1^0.43, with ys scour depth, y1 flow depth upstream of the pier, a pier width, Fr1 = V1/sqrt(g*y1) upstream.
- K1 pier nose shape: square nose 1.1; round nose 1.0 (also circular cylinder 1.0); group of cylinders 1.0; sharp nose about 0.9 (0.9 unverified, the range is 0.9-1.1).
- K2 angle of attack: K2 = (cos(theta) + (L/a) sin(theta))^0.65, with L pier length, L/a capped at 12; K2 = 1 for theta = 0.
- K3 bed condition: clear-water scour 1.1; plane bed and antidune 1.1; small dunes 1.1; medium dunes 1.1-1.2; large dunes 1.3 (older editions K3 1.1-1.3).
- K4 armoring factor (bed material coarse) can reduce scour; K4 between 0.4 and 1.0 for D50 at least 2 mm (only the existence of K4 verified).
- Maximum pier scour depth is limited in practice to about 2.4 a for Fr <= 0.8 and 3.0 a for Fr > 0.8 (from HEC-18, unverified here).
- Contraction scour live-bed (Laursen): y2/y1 = (Q2/Q1)^(6/7) * (W1/W2)^k1, scour = y2 - y0 (k1 0.59-0.69 by mode of transport); clear-water: y2 = [0.025 Q^2 / (Dm^(2/3) W^2)]^(3/7) in SI (from memory).
- Hydrodynamic drag on a pier: F = 0.5 * Cd * rho * V^2 * A (rho = 1000 kg/m3, A = projected area normal to flow = width * depth).
- AASHTO LRFD drag coefficients (from memory, unverified): semicircular-nosed pier 0.7; square-ended pier 1.4; wedge-nosed (angle 90 deg or less) 0.8; debris lodged against pier 1.4. Pressure (MPa) about 5.14e-4 * Cd * V^2 with V in m/s.
- Afflux (Yarnell 1934): dy = 2 K (K + 5 Fr3^2 - 0.6)(alpha + 15 alpha^4) * V3^2/(2g), dy = rise in upstream level; alpha = fraction of channel blocked by piers; Fr3 and V3 at the downstream of the piers.
- Yarnell pier shape K: semicircular nose and tail 0.90; square 1.25; twin cylinders with diaphragm 0.95; 90 deg triangular nose 1.05; multi-column pier 2.5; valid K range 0.7-2.5.
- Buoyancy: submerged masonry weight is reduced by 9.81 kN/m3 (about 10 kN/m3): limestone 26 -> about 16 kN/m3 effective, so sliding and overturning stability of piers drop by about 35-40% when submerged; fill behind spandrels also saturates.
- Usual failure sequence of stone bridges in floods: (1) scour and undermining of pier foundations (the most common cause), (2) debris blockage raising afflux and drag, (3) pier tilt or settlement and abutment spreading, (4) arch ring cracks and hinges, (5) arch or pier collapse, often with spandrel failure first.
- Game numbers: example V = 3 m/s, y = 3 m, a = 3 m, round nose, clear water: Fr = 0.55, ys/y = 2 * 1.0 * 1.0 * 1.1 * (1)^0.65 * 0.55^0.43 = 1.7, ys about 5.1 m (my arithmetic).

Confidence: medium. Caveats: Verified: HEC-18 equation form and K1 square 1.1 / round 1.0, K2 formula, K3 values; Yarnell equation form and K values. NOT verified: sharp-nose K1 0.9, K4 range, scour depth caps 2.4a/3.0a, contraction-scour formulas (from memory), AASHTO drag coefficients (memory), buoyancy numbers (my arithmetic), flood failure sequence (general engineering knowledge). A search summary wrote the Yarnell bracket with 5 Fr rather than 5 Fr^2; the DOT document form with Fr3^2 is the correct one.

Sources: <https://uon.sdsu.edu/onlinescourhec18sampleinput.html>, <https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/infrastructure/hydraulics/12022/002.cfm>, <https://help.floodmodeller.com/docs/pier-loss-bridge>, <https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/14906/dot_14906_DS1.pdf>, <https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9781482288995/chapters/10.1201%2F9781315273426-62>
